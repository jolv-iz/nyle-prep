CPLR Quick Index — Trigger → Answer

Built from diagnostic gaps (Q3, Q5, Q7). Organized by the fact pattern that should make you reach for each rule, not by statute number. Use this during drills — don’t memorize it, get fast at scanning it.

1. SERVICE — “how was the defendant served, and what does that start?”

Trigger in the fact pattern Rule Answer to reach for
“Personally served” (handed papers directly, in NY) CPLR 308(1)/311, 3012(c) Answer due 20 days after service
“Served by mail” / “outside NY” / substituted service CPLR 3012(c) Answer due 30 days after service is complete
“Left with person of suitable age at home/work, then mailed” (deliver-and-mail) CPLR 308(2) Service complete 10 days after filing proof of service — clock for answer runs from that later date, not the hand-off date
“Couldn’t find defendant with due diligence” → posted on door + mailed CPLR 308(4) — affix-and-mail Only available if personal/deliver-and-mail can’t be done with due diligence. This was Q4’s trap — recognize affix-and-mail as the last-resort method.
Personal service by mail with signed acknowledgment CPLR 312-a Answer due 20 days after the signed acknowledgment is mailed/delivered back — not 20 days from receipt

Diagnostic callback (Q4): the exam tests whether you know affix-and-mail is conditional on due diligence failing first — don’t reach for it as a default option.

2. ADDING A PARTY / JOINDER AS OF RIGHT

Trigger Answer
Plaintiff wants to add a new defendant, no court order, original defendant already served Allowed as of right only until the original defendant’s statutory time to answer expires
Original defendant answered early (e.g., answered day 15 of a 20-day window) Deadline is still the full statutory period (20 or 30 days) — early answering doesn’t shorten the joinder window
Time to answer has already expired, or defendant already answered and that period has passed Needs leave of court or stipulation of all parties — no longer “as of right”

Diagnostic callback (Q5 + our drill scenario): the trap is anchoring to when the defendant actually answered rather than when he was statutorily required to. Always calculate the deadline from the statutory clock, not the real-world date of the filed answer.

3. DEFENSES AVAILABLE AFTER A DENIED PRE-ANSWER MOTION TO DISMISS

Trigger Answer
Defendant moved pre-answer to dismiss for failure to state a cause of action, motion denied, now answering May still raise: res judicata, statute of limitations, lack of subject matter jurisdiction in the answer
Same scenario — can defendant still raise lack of personal jurisdiction? No — personal jurisdiction is a defense that’s waived if not raised in that first pre-answer motion (or in the answer if no motion was made)

Diagnostic callback (Q6): the rule to bank is CPLR 3211(e) waiver — personal jurisdiction, and a short list of others (documentary evidence, arbitration/collateral estoppel-type defenses), are “use it or lose it” if you make a pre-answer motion and leave them out. Subject matter jurisdiction, by contrast, can never be waived — it can be raised any time, even on appeal.

4. INTERROGATORIES vs. BILL OF PARTICULARS vs. DEPOSITION (personal injury / negligence)

Trigger Answer
Party wants to serve interrogatories on the same party from whom a bill of particulars has already been demanded, OR who has already been deposed Not allowed without leave of court — pick one discovery-of-that-type tool per party, not both
Party wants interrogatories and neither a BP nor deposition has occurred yet for that party Fine, no leave needed

Diagnostic callback (Q7): this is a mutual exclusivity rule — bill of particulars and deposition are treated as functionally overlapping with interrogatories for this purpose, so either one blocks free-standing interrogatories against the same party. Don’t split this into “BP blocks it, deposition doesn’t” or vice versa — it’s both-or-neither.

5. APPELLATE PRECEDENT ACROSS DEPARTMENTS (Q1 sample — not yet drilled, flagging for review)

Trigger Answer
Trial court in one Judicial Department, only an Appellate Division ruling from a different Department exists on point Binding, unless the Court of Appeals or that trial court’s own Department has ruled to the contrary

Meta-pattern across all of CPLR on this exam

The recurring trap: your instinct picks the “obvious” trigger event (service date, answer date, motion date) but the actual clock runs from a different, more technical event (statutory deadline vs. actual filing; completion of service vs. hand-off; waiver-by-omission vs. waiver-by-silence). When a CPLR question turns on a date or deadline, explicitly ask: “is this measuring from the real-world event, or from the statutory deadline for that event?” — that single question would have flipped 2 of your 3 CPLR misses.

CPLR Civil Action — Procedural Timeline (High Level)

The sequence of a case from filing to judgment. Use this to place any exam fact pattern on the timeline first — most CPLR traps are really “what stage are we at, and what does that stage allow/require.”

STAGE 1 — COMMENCEMENT

Action begins when summons (+ complaint, or summons with notice) is filed with the county clerk.

  • Summons with notice (no complaint yet) → if defendant serves a written demand for the complaint, plaintiff has 20 days to serve it, or face dismissal.
  • Plaintiff then has 120 days from filing to serve the defendant.

STAGE 2 — SERVICE ON DEFENDANT

(→ see CPLR index doc, Section 1, for method-by-method deadlines)

Method When answer clock starts
Personal delivery Service date → 20 days to answer
Deliver-and-mail (person of suitable age + mail) 10 days after filing proof of service → then 20/30-day clock
Affix-and-mail — only if above fails with due diligence Similar delayed-completion logic
Mail outside NY / other means 30 days to answer
Personal service by mail (signed acknowledgment) 20 days from return of signed acknowledgment, not from receipt

STAGE 3 — DEFENDANT’S RESPONSE WINDOW

Defendant must, within the applicable window, either:

(a) Answer, or (b) Make a pre-answer motion — most commonly to dismiss under CPLR 3211.

⚠️ Waiver trap: if defendant moves to dismiss pre-answer and omits certain defenses (notably personal jurisdiction), those defenses are waived — gone for good. Subject matter jurisdiction is never waived this way; it survives to be raised any time.

If the pre-answer motion is denied, defendant must then answer, and may still raise: res judicata, statute of limitations, lack of subject matter jurisdiction — but not the waived defenses.

STAGE 4 — JOINING ADDITIONAL PARTIES

  • Plaintiff may add a party as of right (no court order) only until the original defendant’s statutory time to answer would expire — measured from the statutory deadline, not from when defendant actually answered (even if he answered early).
  • After that window closes → needs leave of court or stipulation.

STAGE 5 — DISCOVERY

Sequence and overlap rules matter more than any single device in isolation:

  • Bill of Particulars — clarifies claims/injuries pled.
  • Depositions (EBT) — oral examination.
  • Interrogatories — written questions. In a personal injury/negligence case, cannot be served on a party without leave of court if a Bill of Particulars has already been demanded of that party, or that party has already been deposed — the two other devices block free-standing interrogatories against the same party.
  • Other devices exist (document demands, physical/mental exams, notices to admit) — flag if you want these added once we hit that diagnostic gap.

STAGE 6 — MOTION PRACTICE (can occur throughout)

  • Pre-answer motions to dismiss (Stage 3).
  • Summary judgment motions (post-discovery, testing whether a triable issue of fact exists).
  • General motion timing: notice at least 8 days before return date; opposition at least 2 days before; reply at least 1 day before (CPLR 2214) — mechanical but occasionally tested.

STAGE 7 — TRIAL

Not typically the CPLR’s focus on NYLE (more of an MBE/general civ pro topic) — NYLE tends to test the procedural mechanics leading up to trial more than trial conduct itself. Flag if sample questions suggest otherwise as we drill more.

STAGE 8 — POST-JUDGMENT / APPEAL

  • Appellate precedent rule: a trial court must follow the Appellate Division of a different Department unless the Court of Appeals, or that trial court’s own Department, has ruled to the contrary. (Not stage-dependent, but worth anchoring here since it governs how any ruling above trial level binds you.)

How to use this under exam pressure

  1. Locate the stage the fact pattern is describing (served? answered? discovery? motion?).
  2. Check the trap category for that stage — Stage 2/4 traps are almost always real event vs. statutory clock; Stage 3 traps are almost always waiver by omission; Stage 5 traps are almost always device overlap/exclusivity.
  3. Only then reach for the specific number/rule.

This mirrors your diagnostic pattern: all three CPLR misses were stage-transition questions (service→answer, answer→joinder, motion→answer), not isolated rule-recall questions.
